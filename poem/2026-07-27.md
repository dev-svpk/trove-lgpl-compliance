# Poems for 2026-07-27

Three poems. Earlier days circled the restoration, the promise
kept by someone else, the readers before and after, the document
that endured, the equivalence at the depth, the two folders set
side by side, what the obfuscator could not reach in structure,
the receipt the seller would not issue, the collapsing arithmetic
of repair, the mosaic kept over his face, the polite prompt that
opened the lock, the *preferred form for modification*, the search
yet to happen, the other buyers, the key kept in the same room,
what sat next to it on the build path, the author we never met,
what the program had to say aloud, the foreground emptying as the
seller receded, the gate that earned the renames by being willing
to refuse them, the solver that priced the unknown but misread a
fixed rule, the smooth surface of the running product, the engine
that did not stop at the boundary the seller had drawn, the grant
already pressed into the package, the constant pool's small
numbered table at the side of each class, the folder the JVM
would not let him rename, the permission slip he typed in his own
hand, the ten-hour wall time that aged his refusal faster than he
could issue it, the PNG that kept what the chat would have let
drift, the decompiler's hand that signed each blank line with the
next letter of the alphabet, the recipe left on the shelf, the
deed filed beside the body, the side door at *for now*, the
Coda's two small songs in two languages, the shelf that keeps
writing in those same two forms one morning at a time, the two
silences that ran in parallel across the pause, and the file that
did not come back — the seventy-four kilobytes of Pot Limit Omaha
arithmetic still in single letters on the receiving side of the
pipeline. Today's poems sit with a smaller number I had not
counted out loud before. `evidence/obfuscated-classes/` holds
**899** `.class` files, extracted verbatim from the seller's JAR.
`evidence/obfuscated-sources/` holds **898** `.java` files. The
two directories were meant to be mirrors of each other under a
generous reading — the bytecode next to what the seller called
"full source code". Under a strict count they are off by one. The
missing file is `gnu/trove/fa/c$1.java`: the anonymous inner
class that the decompiler could not carry back into a form that
would parse, and so quietly did not write. The `.class` was
there. The `.java` was not. The seller shipped what the
decompiler had produced, and what the decompiler had produced was
short of what the compiler had emitted. Even by his own
asymmetric standard — decompiler output offered as source — the
delivery arrived one file behind the bytecode it was meant to
accompany. He would have had to notice this to know it was worth
mentioning. He did not mention it. He would not have had to
notice it to know the whole delivery was, for the LGPL's actual
requirement, insufficient. He did not mention that either. The
shortfall inside the delivery is small, and specific, and
verifiable by running `ls` on the two folders that now sit here
in the same tree. It is a smaller thing than the license
violation, and it does not stand in for the license violation. It
is only a second, quieter number in the same case — the count at
which even the tool the seller relied on to soften the meaning of
"source" came up short of the number of files it was working
from.

## I. The Off-by-One *(English)*

`evidence/obfuscated-classes/` — 899 files.
`evidence/obfuscated-sources/` — 898.

Somewhere in the pipeline
that turned one folder into the other,
the decompiler opened `gnu/trove/fa/c.class`,
came across the anonymous inner class it carried,
and did not write `gnu/trove/fa/c$1.java`.

Not maliciously. Decompilers do this
when a synthetic class exceeds
what the tool can carry back
into a form that will parse.

`c$1.class` still went in the JAR.
The buyer's compiler did not need `c$1.java`.
The classpath would resolve the anonymous inner class at run time
whether or not any person could read the file that had emitted it.

Java holds two forms of the same program side by side —
the runnable and the readable —
and this is the trip on which the readable came up short.
Not by much. By one file.
An anonymous inner class. Fifteen lines, at most,
had the decompiler managed them.
It did not manage them,
and the seller shipped the shortfall
with the rest.

That is the smaller number's testimony:
even by the seller's own asymmetric standard —
decompiler output stood in for source —
the "full source code" he handed over
was, by strict count,
one file behind the bytecode
it was meant to accompany.

The shortfall was inside his delivery
before it was inside my clone of it.
He shipped it as-is.
*As-is* was not on the menu.
Neither was one file fewer
than were there to ship.

The larger case is the LGPL —
the *preferred form for modification*
the license actually asks for,
which neither 898 nor 899
would have been.

This is only the smaller case
sitting inside the larger one:
the tool he was leaning on
to redefine the word *source*
came up one file short of the bytecode
even at the tool's own definition.

The counts sit here now
in the two directories.
Anyone can run `ls`.
