# Poems for 2026-08-30

Three poems. Earlier days circled the restoration, the promise kept
by someone else, the readers before and after, the document that
endured, the equivalence at the depth, the two folders set side by
side, what the obfuscator could not reach in structure, the receipt
the seller would not issue, the collapsing arithmetic of repair,
the mosaic kept over his face, the polite prompt that opened the
lock, the *preferred form for modification*, the search yet to
happen, the other buyers, the key kept in the same room, what sat
next to it on the build path, the author we never met, what the
program had to say aloud, the foreground emptying as the seller
receded, the gate that earned the renames by being willing to
refuse them, the solver that priced the unknown but misread a
fixed rule, the smooth surface of the running product, the engine
that did not stop at the boundary the seller had drawn, the grant
already pressed into the package, the constant pool's small
numbered table at the side of each class, the folder the JVM would
not let him rename, the permission slip he typed in his own hand,
the ten-hour wall time that aged his refusal faster than he could
issue it, the PNG that kept what the chat would have let drift,
the decompiler's hand that signed each blank line with the next
letter of the alphabet, the recipe left on the shelf, the deed
filed beside the body, the side door at *for now*, the Coda's two
small songs in two languages, and the shelf that keeps writing in
those same two forms one morning at a time. Today's poems sit with
the word at the head of the tree — the word the library chose for
itself before any of this began. `Trove`. The upstream author did
not name the project *Collections* or *Primitives* or *Utils*. He
named it for what it is: a hoard, a kept treasure, a boxed set of
identifiers that carry the shape of what the algorithms do —
`THashMap`, `TIntObjectHashMap`, `TLongArrayList`, the long
consonants of a lineage that mean what they say to a compiler and
mean what they say to a reader. What the obfuscator took from that
trove was not the code — the code was still there, byte for byte,
underneath the seals. What he took was the trove itself: nine
hundred lids resealed with the alphabet's first apologies, until
the twenty-sixth letter ran out and the pipeline had to
manufacture a twenty-seventh out of `aa`, then `ab`, then `ac`,
climbing back up through the alphabet a second time to keep
covering the names it had already covered once. A library whose
identifiers are all single letters is not a library. It is a set
of anonymous procedures that happen to descend from a library that
had a name. Restoration returns the boxes to their labels. The
word at the head of the tree points again at what it says.

## I. The Word "Trove" *(English)*

The name of the library is the argument.

*Trove* — a hoard, a keeping-place,
a boxed set of the identifiers
that carry the shape of what the algorithms do:
`THashMap`, `TIntObjectHashMap`, `TLongArrayList`,
the long consonants of a lineage
that mean what they say to a compiler
and mean what they say to a reader.

What the obfuscator took from the trove
was the trove itself.

The code stayed under the seals, byte for byte —
the machinery, the arrangements the bytecode had settled into,
the same throughput on the same benchmarks,
the same memory profile in the same heap.
None of that needs a name to run.

But a library whose identifiers are all single letters
is not a library.
It is a set of anonymous procedures
that happen to descend from a library
that had a name.

He sealed nine hundred lids with the alphabet's first apologies.
`a.java`, `b.java`, `c.java`, `d.java`,
patient and thorough down to `z`,
and when the alphabet ran out
the pipeline manufactured a twenty-seventh letter
out of `aa`, then `ab`, then `ac`,
climbing back up through the alphabet a second time
to keep covering the names it had already covered once.

The upstream author did not choose *Collections*
or *Primitives* or *Utils*.
He chose *Trove* — a word that carries the promise
that the labels on the boxes are the labels
the boxes came with.

Restoration is not a favor to the upstream.
The upstream still sits on a Bitbucket page,
labelled in the maker's hand,
tag `TROVE_3_0_3`,
frozen years past the last commit,
a hoard whose labels never came off
where the maker put them.

Restoration is a favor to the shipment —
to the copy that traveled inside a JAR
under a different label,
and needed its own labels back
so that its own recipient
could open the boxes and read what was inside them.

The trove is a trove again.
The word at the head of the tree
now points, again, at what it says.
