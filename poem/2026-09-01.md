## I. Trove *(English)*

Middle English *trove* — what turns up
when you dig. Coin, chalice, sealed page.
Law calls it *treasure trove*
when no living hand still owns it.

The library at `gnu/trove/`
took the word for its own —
a hoard of small primitive containers,
maps and sets that hold `int` and `long` and `char`
without paying the box tax,
the working silver of a project's inner rooms.

The seller took the word and made it fit
the other, older sense.

His obfuscator drove the coins back into the ground.
`a` laid on top of `THashMap`.
`b` laid on top of `gnu.trove.map.hash`.
`c`, `d`, `e` — a fresh acre of dirt
troweled flat over what a community had minted
and set out in the open for anyone.

He called the surface *full source code*.
It was a field.
Underneath, the hoard was still the hoard.

The equivalence check was the shovel.
The bytecode round-trip was how we knew
we had hit the same coin,
not a coin we had forged in imitation.
Ten hours of unattended machine time
was the excavation.

What came up was what had been put down.
Nothing added.
Nothing minted.
Only the coin turned face-up again
with the mint mark legible:

*GNU Trove 3.0.3, LGPL-2.1,
copyright as inscribed on the rim.*

A trove is not made by finding it.
A trove is made by whoever buried it,
and remains a trove
whether the topsoil is on or off.

But the finder has one duty the burier does not:
to say, at the edge of the pit,
what came out and whose it was.

The shelf is doing that.
The word at the head of the tree
sits inside its own definition now —

a hoard put away,
a hoard uncovered,
a name given back to what was found.
