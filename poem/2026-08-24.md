# Poems for 2026-08-24

Three poems. Earlier days circled the restoration, the promise
kept by someone else, the readers before and after, the document
that endured, the equivalence at the depth, the two folders set
side by side, what the obfuscator could not reach in structure,
the receipt the seller would not issue, the collapsing arithmetic
of repair, the mosaic kept over his face, the polite prompt that
opened the lock, the *preferred form for modification*, the search
yet to happen, the other buyers, the key kept in the same room,
what sat next to it on the build path, the author we never met,
what the program had to say aloud, the foreground emptying as the
seller receded, the gate that earned the renames by being willing
to refuse them, the solver that priced the unknown but misread a
fixed rule, the smooth surface of the running product, the engine
that did not stop at the boundary the seller had drawn, the grant
already pressed into the package, the constant pool's small
numbered table at the side of each class, the folder the JVM
would not let him rename, the permission slip he typed in his
own hand, the ten-hour wall time that aged his refusal faster
than he could issue it, the PNG that kept what the chat would
have let drift, the decompiler's hand that signed each blank line
with the next letter of the alphabet, the recipe left on the
shelf, the deed filed beside the body, the side door at *for
now*, the Coda's two small songs in two languages, the shelf
that keeps writing in those same two forms one morning at a
time, the one file that did not come back, the thirty-three-day
gap, and the two silences running in parallel. Today's poems
turn away from the interval and back to the mechanism. A `.class`
file, before it declares anything else, has to declare that it
*is* a class file — four bytes of magic, then a version, then a
constant pool, then access flags, then this-class and super-class
by index into the pool, then the interfaces, then the fields,
each with a name and a descriptor, then the methods, each with a
name and a descriptor and, for the ones that are not abstract,
their code. None of these can be removed. The JVM will refuse a
file that does not have them. The obfuscator was allowed to
rename what those names *said* — `gnu.trove.map.hash.TIntIntHashMap`
could become `a.b.c.d.e` — but he was not allowed to remove the
fact that a name sat in that slot. The five components of the
original still stood as five components in the mangled version.
The method that used to be `put` was now called `k`, but `k`
still took two ints, returned an int, and jumped to the same
rehash primitive at the same offset in the method table. The
descriptors did not lie: `(II)I` was still `(II)I`. Between the
JVM's format requirements and the reference copy of upstream
Trove sitting on a public server for over a decade, there was
enough left in the file to walk each renamed entry back to its
original name and check the walk by round-tripping the recompile.
The obfuscator was, by construction, only ever painting the
scaffolding. He could not saw through it. The LGPL asks for the
*preferred form for modification*; the JVM asks for the *minimum
form for execution*; between the two, there was more of the
source left in the seller's JAR than he had intended to ship.

## I. What The Format Would Not Give Up *(English)*

Before a `.class` file is allowed to run,
it has to declare that it *is* a class file:
four bytes of magic
at the top of every one.

Then it has to declare a version.
Then a constant pool.
Then access flags.
Then its own name, by index into the pool.
Then its parent's name.
Then the interfaces it implements.
Then, for each field, a name and a descriptor.
Then, for each method, a name and a descriptor,
and, for the ones that are not abstract,
the code.

None of this can be removed.
The JVM refuses a file that does not have it.

The obfuscator was allowed to rename what those names said.
He was not allowed to remove the fact
that a name sat in the slot.

`gnu.trove.map.hash.TIntIntHashMap`
became `a.b.c.d.e` —
but `a.b.c.d.e` still had five components
in exactly the positions the original had five components.
The method that used to be `put` was now called `k`;
`k` still took two ints and returned an int
and still jumped to the rehash primitive
at the same offset in the method table.
The descriptors did not lie.
`(II)I` was still `(II)I`.

Everything the obfuscator could remove, he removed.
Everything the JVM demanded, he left.

What he left was a scaffolding
sufficient to reconstruct the removed part
by referring to the upstream library he had cloned from
and matching, entry by entry,
what the scaffolding said had to be there.

He did not know he had left this map.
He believed he had shipped a lock.
He had shipped, instead,
a lock cast from the same keys that opened it.
Every access flag, every descriptor,
every jump table
was a key stamped into the metal.

The paint over the fence
was the same colour as the fence,
and the fence beneath it
was still a fence made of links.

The LGPL asks for the *preferred form for modification*.
The JVM asks for the *minimum form for execution*.
The seller shipped less than the LGPL demanded.
The JVM demanded more than the seller intended.
Between the two,
there was more of the source
left inside his JAR
than he had ever meant to hand over.

Ten hours of unattended machine time
picked it up.
